Title: Why 40 million Mexicans can't pay their CFE bill online — and what that means for the US diaspora

PASTE THIS AS YOUR OPENING LINES (before "see more" fold — do not change these):
---------------------------------------------------------------------------
My grandmother paid her electricity bill the same way for 40 years.

She'd wake up early, take two buses to the nearest OXXO, stand in line, pay in cash, and keep the paper receipt in a drawer in case there was ever a dispute.

She didn't have a bank account. She didn't have a credit card. She had a phone number and a stack of receipts.

When she died, her drawer was full of them.
---------------------------------------------------------------------------

FULL ARTICLE BODY:
---------------------------------------------------------------------------
What most people outside Mexico don't understand is that she wasn't an exception. She was the rule.


THE INFRASTRUCTURE GAP NOBODY TALKS ABOUT

Mexico's CFE — the national electric utility — serves over 45 million households. To pay your bill online, officially, you need a debit or credit card linked to a Mexican bank account.

According to the INEGI's most recent national financial inclusion survey, 63% of Mexican adults — roughly 60 million people — do not have a bank account. Not because they don't want one. Because the system wasn't built for them: no permanent address history, no formal employment record, no credit bureau file. No file, no account. No account, no online access.

So they pay at OXXO. They pay at 7-Eleven. They stand in line, pay a service fee, and keep the receipt.

Mexico has approximately 20,000 OXXO stores. That sounds like a lot until you consider there are 2,500 municipalities in the country, many of them rural, many of them hours from the nearest convenience store. And every one of those 20,000 stores charges between $9 and $15 pesos per transaction — a fee that hits the people with the least money the hardest, every single month, forever.


WHAT THIS LOOKS LIKE FROM COUNCIL BLUFFS, IOWA

There are 38 million Mexican-born immigrants living in the United States. Many of them are still paying bills in Mexico — a mother's water bill in Monterrey, a sister's CFE in Guadalajara, a family home's internet in Vallarta. They find out the bill is due via WhatsApp. They try to pay online. They hit the bank account wall. They call a cousin to go stand in line. The cousin charges them for the favor, or they wire money and pay the remittance fee on top of the OXXO fee.

This is not a niche problem. It is a structural feature of the Mexican financial system that has been operating the same way for decades — and the people it costs the most are the ones who can least afford to pay it.


WHY FINTECH HASN'T FIXED IT

The honest answer is that most fintech is built for people who already have financial infrastructure. App stores require a credit card. SPEI transfers require a CLABE number, which requires a bank account. "Digital wallets" require a debit card to fund them.

Every product layer assumes the one below it already exists. For the 60 million Mexicans at the bottom — the ones who are cash-economy by necessity, not by choice — each of those assumptions is a wall.

There's also the trust problem. A grandmother who has been managing her household finances in cash for 40 years has a rational reason to distrust an app that asks her to enter her personal information into a phone. She's not being technophobic. She's being smart. Trust isn't given to technology. It's earned, slowly, through reliability and transparency.


WHAT BEHAVIORAL DATA ACTUALLY SHOWS

Here's the thing that gets overlooked in every "financial inclusion" conversation: unbanked people are often extremely reliable payers. They pay their CFE bill because the lights go off if they don't. They pay their water bill because they have children. They pay their phone bill because that phone is their connection to work, to family, to everything.

The consistency of those payments — month after month, service after service — tells a creditworthiness story that no bureau captures because no bureau is looking. There is no FICO score for the woman who has paid her electricity bill without fail for 22 years because she paid it in cash at OXXO.

That gap between observable reliability and recorded creditworthiness is one of the most significant opportunities in financial services today. Not just for inclusion — for accuracy.


WHY I'M WRITING THIS

I spent years watching this problem up close in Puerto Vallarta. The people who needed the most help navigating payments were the ones the system had decided didn't exist.

We built PagoYa because we lived this problem — and because we believed the gap between "reliable payer" and "bankable customer" was not an accident of character. It was an accident of infrastructure.

The infrastructure can change.

Title: What a PTI score actually measures — and why FICO doesn't work for cash-economy workers

PASTE THIS AS YOUR OPENING LINES (before "see more" fold — do not change these):
---------------------------------------------------------------------------
A woman in Guadalajara has paid her electricity bill without fail for nineteen years.

She has never missed a month. She has paid through two recessions, a pandemic, and three changes of government. She budgets for it the way most people budget for rent — because for her family, the lights staying on is not optional.

Her FICO score equivalent: zero. Because she has never had a credit card. Because the system was never watching.
---------------------------------------------------------------------------

FULL ARTICLE BODY:
---------------------------------------------------------------------------
This is not a fringe case. This is the majority of working adults in Mexico.

And it surfaces a problem that the credit industry has known about for decades but never had a clean answer to: FICO — and every bureau-based scoring model built on the same logic — was designed for a financial system that most of the world doesn't use.


WHY FICO FAILS CASH-ECONOMY WORKERS

FICO scores are generated from bureau data. Bureau data comes from lenders. Lenders report to bureaus when someone opens a card, takes a loan, misses a payment, or closes an account.

If you have never done any of those things — because you live in a cash economy, because no one ever offered you a card, because the formal financial system treated you as invisible — you generate no bureau data. No bureau data means no score. No score means no credit. No credit means no score.

It is a perfectly circular trap. And it is not a personal failure. It is a structural one.

The credit gap in Mexico is not a gap in payment discipline. It is a gap in data coverage. The people most locked out of formal credit are frequently the most disciplined payers in the economy — they have to be, because they have no safety net. One missed utility payment means no water. Two missed payments means no electricity. There is no revolving credit line to float them through a hard month.

That discipline is real. It is economically meaningful. And until recently, it was completely invisible.


WHAT PTI ACTUALLY MEASURES

PTI — the Predictive Trust Index — is a behavioral credit score built for people the bureaus have never seen.

It does not ask: "Have you borrowed money before and paid it back?"

It asks something different: "How do you behave with money when no one is offering you a structured product?"

The score runs across four domains:

Payment Reliability — not just whether you pay, but whether you pay early, on time, or under pressure. A payment made three days before the due date tells a different story than one made the same day the lights would go off.

Behavioral Consistency — how stable your financial behavior is over time. Does your deposit timing follow a pattern? Do your payment amounts vary erratically, or are they disciplined and regular? Consistency is a signal that bureaus never capture because bureaus only see events, not patterns between events.

Economic Diversity — how many distinct billers you manage. Someone who pays CFE, water, internet, and a streaming service across a rolling 90-day window is demonstrating something meaningful about their financial organization. That's not the same as someone paying one bill sporadically.

Financial Engagement — how actively you engage with coaching, literacy content, and financial goal-setting. A person who completes a financial literacy module unprompted is signaling something about their future behavior that historical payment data alone cannot.

These four signals combine into a single score. The score is not a FICO replacement — it is a complement, purpose-built for the population FICO was never designed to serve.


THE REGULATORY STRUCTURE MATTERS

PTI is deliberately designed to sit outside Mexico's SIC (credit bureau) authorization framework. It operates as a "predictive indicator of payment capacity" — not a regulated credit score — which means it can be licensed to financial institutions, MFIs, and fintechs without the regulatory overhead that would otherwise slow this by years.

That distinction matters for the people being scored, too. A PTI score is not a judgment about your past. It is a forward-looking signal about your behavior. Those are meaningfully different things, and the distinction is built into how the model is constructed.

We also completed external fair-lending certification in July 2026 — a compensatory gating framework that delivered a 9x improvement in the four-fifths fairness ratio. If a scoring model is going to be used in lending decisions for a population that has historically been excluded, it needs to meet a higher standard of equity than the models that excluded them in the first place.


THE LARGER POINT

Credit scoring is not a neutral technology. Every scoring model embeds assumptions about what financial behavior looks like and whose behavior is worth measuring.

FICO assumed formal employment. It assumed bank accounts. It assumed that creditworthiness expressed itself through institutions. For the people those institutions chose to serve, that worked. For everyone else, it produced a permanent blind spot.

PTI is one answer to that blind spot — built for a specific market, calibrated on real behavioral data, designed from the start with fair-lending constraints baked in. It is not the only answer. But it is a more honest one.

The woman in Guadalajara with nineteen years of on-time electricity payments is not a credit risk. She is a data problem. And data problems, unlike character problems, can be solved.
